\begin{abstract}
How does the cost of accessing alternative finance affect investment and
output for a firm facing a bank-credit limit? A firm finances capital with
internal funds, relatively cheap bank credit up to a limit, and an alternative
source carrying an access wedge. The model derives a threshold equal to the
discounted marginal product at the bank limit minus the bank rate. Above this
threshold, alternative finance is too expensive and the bank limit determines
investment. Below it, the firm invests beyond the limit until marginal product
equals the alternative-finance cost. A larger bank line then changes the
financing mix but not total investment, while a lower access wedge raises
investment and output. A sectoral extension lets firms draw access wedges from
sector-specific distributions, so the constrained share is generated by the
model rather than assigned directly. Preliminary Peruvian forecast evidence
motivates the exercise, but the model does not derive forecast accuracy,
causality, or exact horizons.
\end{abstract}

\noindent\textbf{Keywords:} credit limits; alternative finance; investment;
financing substitution; sectoral heterogeneity.\\
\textbf{JEL codes:} E22, E32, E44, G21, G32.

\section{Introduction}

Consider a firm that has exhausted its bank-credit line but still has a
productive investment opportunity. It can stop at the bank limit or pay more
to raise funds elsewhere. The relevant question is not simply whether another
source exists. It is whether the return to the next unit of capital is high
enough to cover the additional cost of accessing that source.

This paper asks: how does the cost of accessing alternative finance affect
investment and output for a firm facing a bank-credit limit? The question
distinguishes two objects often treated as interchangeable. Bank-credit
availability determines how far a firm can invest at the cheap rate. The
access wedge determines whether it is profitable to continue beyond that
limit.

The model contains one firm and one continuous access wedge. The firm has
internal funds, bank credit at rate $\Rb$ up to $\bar b$, and alternative
finance at cost $\Rb+\tau$. Production is increasing and strictly concave. The
firm's marginal financing cost is therefore step-shaped, while its marginal
product declines with installed capital.

The main result is a threshold proposition. Let $\bar k=n+\bar b$ be capital at
the bank limit. For a firm that would like to invest beyond $\bar k$ at the bank
rate, the critical access wedge is
\[
\tau^*=\beta F'(\bar k)-\Rb.
\]
When $\tau\ge\tau^*$, alternative finance is too expensive and the firm stops
at $\bar k$. When $\tau<\tau^*$, the firm invests beyond the bank limit until
discounted marginal product equals $\Rb+\tau$. The policy is continuous but
kinked at the threshold.

Heterogeneity and sectors enter only after the firm result is established.
Firms draw access wedges from a sector-specific distribution $G_s$. The share
whose investment is governed by the bank limit is then
$1-G_s(\tau^*)$. This replaces the earlier practice of assigning a constrained
share directly and makes the empirical assumption explicit: sectors differ in
the distribution of access costs, not because every firm in a sector is one
financing type.

Fernandez and Chavez (2026) provide the motivation for studying this
heterogeneity. Their preliminary Peruvian forecasting exercise suggests that
credit volume and a credit spread may contain different information across
tradable and non-tradable activity. That pattern is not the object proved in
this paper. Observed credit volume is an imperfect proxy for credit
availability, and the measured bank spread is an imperfect proxy for the
marginal financing cost. A static firm problem also cannot rank forecast
horizons or establish causality.

The contribution is therefore deliberately narrow: a transparent access-cost
threshold, its investment and output comparative statics, and a distributional
bridge to sectoral heterogeneity. This question matches what the model can
prove by hand, reproduce numerically, and formalize in Lean.

\section{Related literature and positioning}

The first strand studies equilibrium credit rationing. Stiglitz and Weiss
(1981) show that asymmetric information can prevent the interest rate from
clearing the loan market, so observationally similar borrowers may face a
quantity restriction. Petersen and Rajan (1994) find that lending relationships
benefit small firms primarily through greater availability of funds rather
than large price reductions. The quantity regime below takes a credit ceiling
as a reduced-form representation of those frictions; it does not reproduce
their screening or relationship formation models.

The second strand studies balance-sheet and collateral amplification.
Kiyotaki and Moore (1997) make durable assets both productive inputs and
collateral, generating persistent responses through endogenous credit limits.
Bernanke, Gertler, and Gilchrist (1999) embed an external-finance premium and
heterogeneous capital-market access in a dynamic macroeconomic framework.
Holmstrom and Tirole (1997) link firm net worth and intermediary capital to the
supply of loanable funds. The present paper treats net worth and the credit
ceiling as predetermined in its baseline. Section~\ref{sec:extensions} shows
how an endogenous ceiling can recover an accelerator without obscuring the
main regime result.

The third strand establishes firm heterogeneity. Gertler and Gilchrist (1994)
document that small firms contract more sharply after monetary tightening,
while Petersen and Rajan (1994) emphasize their dependence on institutional
lenders. These findings motivate a higher constrained share among
non-tradable firms. This is a composition assumption to be disciplined by
data, not a theorem that every small firm is non-tradable or every tradable
firm has market access.

The fourth strand concerns leading financial indicators. Gilchrist and
Zakrajsek (2012) construct a credit-spread measure with predictive power for
future activity, and Faust et al. (2013) find that credit spreads improve
real-time activity forecasts. Stock and Watson (2003) caution that predictive
performance can be unstable across countries and periods. For Peru, Lahura
(2011) finds a stable long-run credit-output relationship, while Butron and
Pozo (2025) document a positive relationship using sectoral and regional
heterogeneity.

The closest paper for the sectoral motivation is Muller and Verner (2024).
They show that credit allocation toward non-tradable rather than tradable
sectors predicts different macroeconomic outcomes, discuss smaller and more
constrained non-tradable firms, and report robustness involving bond issuance.
That evidence means neither the tradable/non-tradable split nor financing
heterogeneity can be claimed as novel here. Holmstrom and Tirole's (1994)
working-paper version and their published 1997 model also already contain
capital thresholds associated with monitored and direct finance. Gilchrist,
Sim, and Zakrajsek (2014) subsequently model the joint dynamics of investment
and credit spreads.

The narrower course-paper contribution is to derive an explicit access-cost
threshold within a capped-bank financing problem and use it to generate the
constrained share from a distribution of firm wedges. The threshold separates
a real quantity response from pure financing substitution. This is a
provisional positioning claim, not a claim that credit rationing, external
finance, or the broader empirical sector pattern is new.

\section{Environment}

There are two dates, $t$ and $t+1$. A firm enters date $t$ with internal funds
$n>0$. It chooses capital $k$, bank borrowing $b$, and alternative finance $m$.
Capital becomes productive at $t+1$, linking the current financing decision to
next-period production.

\subsection{Technology}

The general model uses a differentiable production function $F:\R_+\to\R_+$.

\begin{assumption}[Technology]\label{ass:technology}
$F(0)=0$, $F'(k)>0$, and $F''(k)<0$ on the economically relevant domain.
Future revenue is $F(k)$ and its date-$t$ value is $\beta F(k)$, where
$\beta\in(0,1]$.
\end{assumption}

Closed forms and the Lean verification use
\begin{equation}\label{eq:quadratic}
F(k)=ak-\frac{c}{2}k^2,\qquad a>0,\quad c>0,
\end{equation}
restricted to $k<a/c$, where output is increasing. This quadratic form is a
local second-order representation of any smooth concave technology and keeps
all regime boundaries transparent.

\subsection{Financing}

The firm's sources satisfy
\begin{equation}\label{eq:financeidentity}
k=n+b+m,\qquad 0\le b\le \bar b,\qquad m\ge 0.
\end{equation}
Bank finance has gross marginal cost $\Rb>0$. Alternative finance costs
\begin{equation}
\Rm(\tau)=\Rb+\tau,\qquad \tau\ge 0.
\end{equation}
The access wedge $\tau$ includes issuance costs, information costs, and other
barriers to non-bank funding. A very high wedge makes the alternative source
economically irrelevant without requiring a separate binary access type.

\begin{assumption}[Funding order]\label{ass:fundingorder}
$0<\Rb\le\Rm<\beta F'(0)$. The firm uses internal funds first, bank credit
second, and alternative finance last. The strict upper bound guarantees a
positive desired scale. The equations $\beta F'(k)=\Rb$ and
$\beta F'(k)=\Rm$ are assumed to have solutions in the economically relevant
domain; strict concavity makes those solutions unique.
\end{assumption}

Empirically, the observed corporate loan spread is only a candidate proxy for
$\tau$ or $\Rm$. The model does not assume that a bank spread literally equals
the cost of bonds or foreign credit. Section~\ref{sec:extensions} discusses
this identification boundary.

\section{The firm problem}

The firm maximizes discounted profits:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:firmproblem}
\max_{k,b,m}\quad \beta F(k)-\Rb b-\Rm m
\quad\text{s.t.}\quad \eqref{eq:financeidentity}.
\end{equation}
Let the capital stock attainable after exhausting the bank line be
\begin{equation}
\bar k\equiv n+\bar b.
\end{equation}
Because bank finance is weakly cheaper, the reduced problem is continuous and
piecewise:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:piecewiseprofit}
\Pi(k)=
\begin{cases}
\beta F(k)-\Rb(k-n), & n\le k\le\bar k,\\[3pt]
\beta F(k)-\Rb\bar b-\Rm(k-\bar k), & k>\bar k.
\end{cases}
\end{equation}
The left and right first-order conditions are
\begin{align}
\beta F'(k)&=\Rb &&\text{(bank-finance margin)},\label{eq:focbank}\\
\beta F'(k)&=\Rm &&\text{(alternative-finance margin)}.\label{eq:focmarket}
\end{align}
Let $\kb$ and $\km$ solve \eqref{eq:focbank} and
\eqref{eq:focmarket}. Strict concavity makes both targets unique. Since
$\Rm\ge\Rb$, declining marginal product implies $\km\le\kb$.

Under \eqref{eq:quadratic},
\begin{equation}\label{eq:targets}
\kb=\frac{\beta a-\Rb}{\beta c},
\qquad
\km=\frac{\beta a-\Rm}{\beta c},
\qquad \km\le\kb.
\end{equation}

\begin{lemma}[Optimal capital with alternative finance]\label{lem:policy}
Under Assumptions~\ref{ass:technology}--\ref{ass:fundingorder}, the unique
optimal capital stock is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:policy}
k^*=\begin{cases}
n, & \kb\le n,\\
\kb, & n<\kb<\bar k,\\
\bar k, & \km\le\bar k\le\kb,\\
\km, & \bar k<\km.
\end{cases}
\end{equation}
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
On $[n,\bar k]$, the derivative of profit is
$\beta F'(k)-\Rb$; on $(\bar k,\infty)$ it is
$\beta F'(k)-\Rm$. Both are strictly decreasing. If $\kb\le n$, the
left derivative is already non-positive at $n$. If
$n<\kb<\bar k$, it crosses zero at $\kb$ before the kink. If
$\km\le\bar k\le\kb$, the derivative is weakly positive immediately to the
left of the kink and weakly negative immediately to its right, so the kink is
optimal. Finally, if $\bar k<\km$, profit continues increasing after the bank
line is exhausted and reaches its maximum at $\km$. Strict concavity gives
uniqueness, with adjacent formulas agreeing at equality boundaries.
\end{proof}

\section{The access-cost threshold}

Focus on the economically relevant case in which the firm would like to invest
beyond the bank limit at the bank rate:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:focal-assumption}
\beta F'(\bar k)>\Rb.
\end{equation}
Define the access-cost threshold
\begin{equation}\label{eq:tau-star}
\tau^*\equiv\beta F'(\bar k)-\Rb>0.
\end{equation}

\begin{proposition}[Access threshold, investment, and output]\label{prop:regimes}
Under Assumptions~\ref{ass:technology}--\ref{ass:fundingorder} and
\eqref{eq:focal-assumption}:
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\roman*)]
\item If $\tau\ge\tau^*$, alternative finance is too expensive and
$k^*=\bar k=n+\bar b$.
\item If $0\le\tau<\tau^*$, the firm uses alternative finance and its unique
optimum satisfies
\begin{equation}\label{eq:alternative-foc}
\beta F'(k^*)=\Rb+\tau,\qquad k^*>\bar k.
\end{equation}
\item Within strict regimes,
\begin{align}
\tau>\tau^*:
&&\frac{\partial k^*}{\partial\bar b}=1,
&&\frac{\partial k^*}{\partial\tau}=0,
\label{eq:quantityeffects}\\
0\le\tau<\tau^*:
&&\frac{\partial k^*}{\partial\bar b}=0,
&&\frac{\partial k^*}{\partial\tau}
=\frac{1}{\beta F''(k^*)}<0.
\label{eq:priceeffects-general}
\end{align}
Output $y_{t+1}=F(k^*)$ has the corresponding signs by the chain rule.
\end{enumerate}
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
At $\bar k$, the derivative of profit immediately to the left is positive by
\eqref{eq:focal-assumption}. The right derivative is
\[
\Pi'_+(\bar k)=\beta F'(\bar k)-(\Rb+\tau)=\tau^*-\tau.
\]
If $\tau\ge\tau^*$, the slope changes from positive to non-positive at the
kink, so strict concavity makes $\bar k$ the unique optimum. If
$\tau<\tau^*$, profit continues increasing beyond $\bar k$ and strict
concavity gives the unique solution to \eqref{eq:alternative-foc}. Implicitly
differentiating that condition yields
$\beta F''(k^*)\,\partial k^*/\partial\tau=1$. The remaining derivatives
follow directly from $k^*=n+\bar b$ in the constrained regime and from the
independence of \eqref{eq:alternative-foc} from $\bar b$ in the
alternative-finance regime.
\end{proof}

For the quadratic technology,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:quadraticcs}
\tau^*=\beta\bigl[a-c(n+\bar b)\bigr]-\Rb,
\qquad
\km(\tau)=\frac{\beta a-\Rb-\tau}{\beta c},
\end{equation}
so, within the focal domain,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:quadratic-policy}
k^*(\tau)=\max\{n+\bar b,\km(\tau)\},
\qquad
\frac{\partial\km}{\partial\tau}=-\frac{1}{\beta c}.
\end{equation}
More directly, the amount invested beyond the bank limit is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:investment-gap}
k^*(\tau)-\bar k
=\max\left\{0,\frac{\tau^*-\tau}{\beta c}\right\}.
\end{equation}
Thus the distance below the threshold maps linearly into additional capital.
If $\Delta k\equiv k^*-\bar k$, the associated output gain is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:output-gap}
F(k^*)-F(\bar k)
=\Delta k\bigl[a-c\bar k\bigr]-\frac{c}{2}(\Delta k)^2>0
\end{equation}
whenever $\Delta k>0$ and both capital stocks remain in the increasing region.

\begin{remark}[Local zero effects]
The zero derivatives are regime statements, not universal claims. A sufficiently
large change in $\tau$ can move a firm across $\tau^*$, and a sufficiently
large increase in the bank ceiling changes the threshold itself. The model
therefore predicts a continuous but kinked policy rather than universal zero
effects.
\end{remark}

\section{Sectoral aggregation and empirical mapping}

Let firms differ in their access wedges. In sector $s\in\{N,T\}$, let
$\tau_i$ have a continuous cumulative distribution $G_s$. Holding the remaining primitives
fixed, Proposition~\ref{prop:regimes} generates the constrained share:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:constrained-share}
\lambda_s
\equiv\Pr(\tau_i\ge\tau^*)
=1-G_s(\tau^*).
\end{equation}
This is an outcome of the threshold model, not a separate assignment of firms
to binary financing types.

Sectoral output can be written as
\begin{equation}\label{eq:aggregate}
Y_{s,t+1}
=\int_{0}^{\tau^*}F\bigl(\km(\tau)\bigr)\,dG_s(\tau)
+\bigl[1-G_s(\tau^*)\bigr]F(\bar k).
\end{equation}
The first term collects firms that use alternative finance; the second collects
firms stopped by the bank limit. Because $\km(\tau^*)=\bar k$, output is
continuous at the boundary.

If the non-tradable distribution is shifted toward higher access wedges, then
$1-G_N(\tau^*)>1-G_T(\tau^*)$: more non-tradable firms have investment pinned
to bank availability. A distribution shifted toward lower wedges contains more
firms whose scale is governed by the cost of alternative finance. This is a
testable hypothesis about $G_N$ and $G_T$, not a theorem that every firm in one
sector is constrained.

The mechanism suggests three empirical checks. First, credit-quantity effects
should be stronger among firms with exhausted bank lines and high access
wedges. Second, financing-cost effects should be stronger among firms already
using bonds, foreign credit, or other non-bank sources. Third, a larger bank
line should raise total finance for firms above the threshold but mainly change
debt composition for firms below it. These interactions are sharper tests than
a mechanical mapping from the static model to forecast horizons.

\section{Numerical illustration}

Consider $\beta=0.95$, $a=2$, $c=0.5$, $n=0.4$,
$\bar b=0.5$, and $\Rb=0.5$. Then
\begin{equation}
\bar k=0.9,\qquad \kb=2.947,\qquad \tau^*=0.9725.
\end{equation}
With $\tau=1.10$, alternative finance is too expensive and the firm stops at
$k=0.9$. With $\tau=0.90$, the alternative-finance target is $k=1.053$, so the
firm uses $0.153$ of alternative finance after exhausting its bank line.

\section{Extensions and limitations}\label{sec:extensions}

\paragraph{Endogenous bank ceiling.}
Let $\bar b_{i,t}=\phi_s n_{i,t}$, with pledgeability $\phi_s>0$. In the
constrained regime, $k_{i,t}=(1+\phi_s)n_{i,t}$, so a net-worth shock changes
investment directly and through borrowing capacity. Adding
$n_{i,t+1}=\omega\Pi_{i,t}$ creates a simple financial accelerator. This
extension is useful for propagation, but not necessary for the core
access-threshold proposition.

\paragraph{Finite alternative-finance capacity.}
The baseline lets a firm raise any amount at marginal cost $\Rb+\tau_i$. A
finite limit $\bar m_i$ would add a second quantity constraint. The current
paper omits it because the empirical question concerns whether the first unit
beyond the bank limit is financed; adding another cap would not change the
threshold for that first unit.

\paragraph{Dynamic capital and horizons.}
A disciplined dynamic version introduces
$K_{t+1}=(1-\delta)K_t+I_t$, a convex installation cost $\Phi_s(I_t)$, and
the same two financing tranches for investment.  Let $q_{i,t}$ denote the
current-value shadow price of installed capital.  The investment and capital
first-order conditions imply
\begin{align}
 q_{i,t}
   &= R_{j,t}+\Phi_s'(I_{i,t})+\mu_{i,t},
   \label{eq:q-investment}\\
 q_{i,t}
   &= \beta\E_t\!\left[
       F_s'(K_{i,t+1})+(1-\delta)q_{i,t+1}
     \right],
   \label{eq:q-capital}
\end{align}
where $j=B$ when the marginal unit is bank financed and $j=M$ when it uses
alternative finance.  The term $\mu_{i,t}\geq0$ is the shadow wedge attached to a
binding quantity limit; it is zero away from that limit (with the bank/market
cost gap instead determining which tranche is marginal).  Combining
\eqref{eq:q-investment}--\eqref{eq:q-capital} yields the Euler equation
\begin{equation}
 R_{j,t}+\Phi_s'(I_{i,t})+\mu_{i,t}
 =\beta\E_t\!\left[
 F_s'(K_{i,t+1})+(1-\delta)
 \{R_{j',t+1}+\Phi_s'(I_{i,t+1})+\mu_{i,t+1}\}
 \right].
 \label{eq:dynamic-euler}
\end{equation}
Thus a credit-quantity innovation operates through the current and expected
shadow wedges, whereas an access-cost innovation changes the marginal
financing price directly. Sector-specific curvature in $\Phi_s$, depreciation, or
project gestation lags can shift the output response across horizons and may
rationalize why preliminary quantity evidence appears at relatively short
horizons while price information appears over a longer horizon. Estimating
those dynamic parameters is left outside the current proposition so that the
first deliverable remains exactly provable by hand; equation
\eqref{eq:dynamic-euler} states the disciplined route for the next version
rather than claiming to identify the observed horizons already.

\paragraph{Measurement.}
The model's $\tau$ is an access wedge, while the thesis uses a bank loan spread
assigned by firm size because sector-level rates are unavailable. The empirical
proxy may therefore contain measurement error and need not move one for one
with bond or foreign-credit costs. A stronger empirical design would test
whether the spread co-moves with funding substitution or use direct measures of
bond, foreign-credit, or non-bank access.

\paragraph{Boundaries of the result.}
The model abstracts from default, risk choice, lender optimization, general
equilibrium, and feedback from future expected output to current credit supply.
It explains a conditional transmission mechanism, not the origin of the
credit ceiling or the causal identification of credit shocks.

\section{Conclusion}

The cost of alternative finance determines whether a bank-credit limit becomes
a real investment constraint. Above $\tau^*$, the firm stops at the limit and
additional bank availability raises investment and future output. Below
$\tau^*$, the firm finances the marginal unit elsewhere; a larger bank line
changes the source of funds but not the scale of investment, while the access
wedge governs that scale. Sectoral differences arise from distributions of
$\tau_i$, not from assigning every firm in a sector to one financing type.

This mechanism turns the thesis's preliminary sectoral pattern into a formal
economic hypothesis with explicit assumptions, first-order conditions, regime
boundaries, and falsifiable cross-sectional predictions. Its value for the
course is not that it proves the forecasting result, but that it supplies the
firm problem the empirical exercise previously treated as a black box.

\section{Additional derivations}

For the quadratic technology, profit on a segment with marginal funding cost
$R$ is, up to constants,
\begin{equation}
\pi_R(k)=\beta\left(ak-\frac c2k^2\right)-Rk.
\end{equation}
The FOC and second-order condition are
\begin{equation}
\pi_R'(k)=\beta(a-ck)-R=0,\qquad
\pi_R''(k)=-\beta c<0.
\end{equation}
Completing the square gives the global identity
\begin{equation}
\pi_R(k_R)-\pi_R(k)=\frac{\beta c}{2}(k-k_R)^2\ge0,
\end{equation}
where $k_R=(\beta a-R)/(\beta c)$. At the bank ceiling, global optimality in
the kink regime follows from
\begin{align}
\Pi(\bar k)-\Pi(k)
&=(\bar k-k)\bigl[\beta F'(\bar k)-\Rb\bigr]
+\frac{\beta c}{2}(\bar k-k)^2\ge0,
&&k\le\bar k,\\
\Pi(\bar k)-\Pi(k)
&=(k-\bar k)\bigl[\Rm-\beta F'(\bar k)\bigr]
+\frac{\beta c}{2}(k-\bar k)^2\ge0,
&&k\ge\bar k.
\end{align}
These are the identities formalized by the Lean theorem
\texttt{kink\_regime\_optimal}.

\section{Cobb-Douglas robustness}

If $F(k)=Ak^\alpha$ with $A>0$ and $0<\alpha<1$, the two unconstrained targets
are
\begin{equation}
k_j=\left(\frac{\beta\alpha A}{R_j}\right)^{\frac{1}{1-\alpha}},
\qquad j\in\{B,M\}.
\end{equation}
The policy function retains exactly the four cases in
Lemma~\ref{lem:policy}. In the alternative-finance regime,
\begin{equation}
\frac{\partial k_M}{\partial\tau}
=-\frac{k_M}{(1-\alpha)R_M}<0,
\qquad
\frac{\partial F(k_M)}{\partial\tau}
=-\frac{\alpha}{1-\alpha}\frac{F(k_M)}{R_M}<0.
\end{equation}
Thus the regime ranking is not an artifact of the quadratic technology.
